Tiresias' prohibition was the last one they broke. On the island of Thrinacia
the crew, pinned down by a month of contrary winds and out of supplies,
slaughtered the cattle of Helios while Odysseus slept. The Sun demanded
payment from Zeus, and the first thunderbolt broke the ship into splinters.
Every man drowned. For nine days the sea carried Odysseus on a piece of the
keel, and threw him onto Ogygia.

Calypso, a nymph on an island in the middle of an empty ocean, picked him up,
nursed him and offered what had never been offered to any mortal before him:
stay forever, never age, never die. Husband to a goddess, on an island where
nothing hurts.

He lived there seven years. The nights he spent in the cave, because there was
nowhere else to go; the days he spent on a rock at the cliff edge, looking at
the sea and weeping. He was being offered eternity, and he wanted to go back
to a stony island the size of a palm, to an ageing wife and a son he had last
seen as an infant.

When the gods finally ordered her to release him, Calypso asked him outright:
in what way is a mortal Penelope better than she is? He answered honestly — in
no way. And he left anyway. That answer is the whole Odyssey: going home is
worth more than immortality, not because home is better, but because it is
his.
