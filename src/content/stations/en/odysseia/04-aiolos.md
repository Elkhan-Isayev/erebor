Aeolus, keeper of the winds, took them in as friends and gave a parting gift
nobody else had ever received: he shut every contrary gale into a leather sack
and left only the west wind loose. The way home turned into a straight line.

For nine days and nights Odysseus never let go of the steering oar and never
closed his eyes — he trusted the sack to no one. On the tenth day Ithaca rose
on the horizon, close enough to make out smoke and the fires on the beach. And
then he slept.

The crew had been staring at that sack for nine days. They were certain their
captain was dividing the spoils unfairly and hiding the gold of Troy inside
it. They untied the knot in sight of home. The escaping winds flattened the
sails and threw the ships back in one motion — all the way to the island of
Aeolus. This time the keeper of the winds drove them off his threshold: a man
so plainly hated by the gods is not a man you help. The closest Odysseus ever
came to home, he came in his sleep.
