The strait was a bowshot wide. On the left, in a cave in the sheer rock, sat
Scylla: six serpent necks, six mouths. On the right, Charybdis swallowed the
sea three times a day and spat it back, and whatever went into the funnel went
whole.

Circe explained the arithmetic without offering any comfort. Hug Charybdis and
the ship dies with every man aboard. Pass under Scylla's rock and she takes
six — one per mouth. And she added the thing that mattered: do not try to
fight. Start defending yourself and you lose another six.

Odysseus steered under the rock. He told the crew nothing: men who know that
six of them have already been assigned as the price do not row. While everyone
watched the whirlpool on the right, six shadows dropped from above. He heard
them calling his name, and he held his course.

It is the heaviest passage in the poem, and the most adult. There is no clever
trick here that saves everybody. There is only the price paid by the one who
decides for other people.
