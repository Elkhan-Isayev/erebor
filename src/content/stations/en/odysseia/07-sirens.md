Circe had warned him in advance: on that island there is singing, and whoever
hears it turns towards the shore, and on the shore lies a heap of bones and
dried skin. She proposed a simple solution — stop every ear with wax and sail
past.

Odysseus followed half her advice. He stopped every ear but his own, and had
himself lashed to the mast, with orders given in advance: however he begged or
threatened or commanded, the ropes were only to be pulled tighter.

The Sirens did not sing about love. They sang that they knew everything that
had happened under Troy, and everything that happens anywhere on earth — and
they offered to let him stay and find out. For a man who had agreed to lose
ten years of his home in order to understand everything and outthink everyone,
it was precisely aimed: not pleasure, but the final answer. He tore at the
ropes and shouted to be released. The oarsmen heard nothing and pulled the
knots tighter.

He remained the only man who heard that song and sailed away. The price was
knowing, for the rest of his life, exactly what he had failed to learn.
